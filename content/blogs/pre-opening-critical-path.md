A hotel can look finished and still be nowhere near ready to operate.

The rooms are handed over, the lobby lighting works and the opening date is on the calendar. Yet the management team is still recruiting, suppliers are being approved, the PMS is only partly configured and department heads are trying to train new employees inside a building that is still being snagged.

This is where many first-year problems begin.

Pre-opening is not the period between construction and launch. It is an operating project in its own right. The strongest openings are planned backwards from the target date, with enough time for people, systems, supply chains, commercial activity and operational testing to mature before the first paying guest arrives.

For owners, five decisions deserve particular attention.

## 1. Hiring the leadership team early enough

A general manager, director of finance, director of sales, chief engineer and key department heads are not simply employees who need to be in place by opening day. They are part of the opening process itself.

They need time to recruit their teams, establish standards, test procedures, build local relationships and challenge assumptions made during development.

When senior hires arrive late, the consequences spread quickly. Recruitment becomes rushed. Training is compressed. Department heads inherit decisions they did not help make. Suppliers are selected under pressure. Commercial teams start selling a hotel they have barely had time to position.

The owner question should therefore be more specific than, “When do we need the GM?”

Ask instead: **What decisions must this person influence before opening, and how much lead time do those decisions require?**

That changes recruitment from a staffing task into a critical-path item.

## 2. Treating systems implementation as an operating exercise

A property-management system, point-of-sale platform, finance system, payroll setup, procurement process and reporting structure can all be technically installed and still be operationally unready.

Configuration is only the beginning.

Room types must match the inventory. Rate plans need to be structured properly. User permissions need to reflect real responsibilities. Interfaces must be tested. Reports have to reconcile. Teams need to understand what happens when a reservation changes, a payment fails, a guest moves rooms, a charge is disputed or a night audit does not balance.

The best time to discover those issues is during controlled testing, not during the first busy weekend.

Owners should ask for a clear systems-readiness plan that separates four stages: installation, configuration, integration and live-user testing. If all four are being discussed as one milestone, the programme is probably too optimistic.

## 3. Protecting training from construction delay

Training is one of the easiest activities to compress because it appears flexible on a programme.

It is not.

A delayed guestroom floor, kitchen or restaurant often pushes practical training into the final days before opening. New employees may learn procedures in meeting rooms, practise on temporary equipment or receive brand training without enough time to apply it in the actual workplace.

That creates a predictable gap between knowing a standard and being able to deliver it under pressure.

A better pre-opening plan protects a period for operational rehearsals. Run arrivals. Turn rooms. Receive stock. Execute a banquet setup. Process a complaint. Close a restaurant shift. Complete night audit. Test emergency procedures. Make the mistakes while the hotel is still allowed to make them privately.

A soft opening can help, but it should validate readiness rather than replace training that never happened.

## 4. Ordering OS&E from the operation backwards

Operating supplies and equipment are rarely glamorous, but missing them can stop a department just as effectively as missing a major piece of plant.

The challenge is not simply placing purchase orders. It is establishing specifications, quantities, approval routes, delivery windows, storage plans, installation responsibilities and replacement stock.

This becomes more difficult when imported items, brand approvals or specialist equipment are involved.

The practical test is simple: follow a guest journey and a staff journey through the building. What does each department physically need to deliver the service promise at every step? Then trace each item backwards to specification, procurement, delivery, storage and issue.

A spreadsheet showing that an item has been “ordered” is not the same as operational readiness.

## 5. Starting the commercial engine before the doors open

Opening day should not be the day the hotel begins looking for demand.

Corporate accounts, groups, events, travel partners, digital channels, local partnerships and rate strategy all need lead time. The commercial team also needs a realistic understanding of what the hotel will actually be ready to sell.

This is especially important when the construction programme is still moving. Selling inventory or facilities before handover dates are credible can create immediate service-recovery problems and damage confidence with the very accounts the hotel needs to retain.

Revenue management should therefore sit inside the pre-opening conversation, not after it. Room types, opening inventory, channel availability, launch pricing, group displacement rules and forecast assumptions should be aligned before sales activity accelerates.

## The opening date is not the only date that matters

Owners naturally focus on the public opening date because it is visible, expensive and often linked to financing, brand commitments or wider development milestones.

But a hotel has several internal opening dates before that one: the date the leadership team must be in place, the date recruitment must accelerate, the date systems enter testing, the date procurement freezes, the date practical training begins and the date the commercial team needs sellable inventory.

If one of those dates slips, the public opening can still happen. The cost is simply transferred into the first year through overtime, service recovery, weak controls, rushed purchasing, staff turnover and lost revenue opportunities.

That is why a good critical path does not ask only, “Can we open?”

It asks, **“Can we operate properly from the day we open?”**

For an owner, that is the more valuable question.

## Five questions to put into the next pre-opening meeting

1. Which leadership roles are currently on the critical path, and what decisions are waiting for them?
2. Which systems have been live-tested by the people who will actually use them?
3. How much protected time remains for practical training inside completed operational areas?
4. Which OS&E or supplier items could realistically delay a department from operating at full standard?
5. What business is already being sold, and does the operational programme support those commitments?

A successful opening is not a single event. It is the result of hundreds of decisions reaching maturity at roughly the same time. The earlier those dependencies are made visible, the less the owner has to pay for them later.
