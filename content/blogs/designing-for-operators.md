Hotel design conversations naturally gravitate towards what the guest will see.

The arrival sequence matters. The lobby has to feel right. Restaurants need identity. Bedrooms must be attractive, efficient and commercially sensible.

But some of the most expensive design mistakes happen in spaces the guest never sees.

A badly located service lift, undersized receiving area, missing housekeeping store or awkward route between kitchen and ballroom can create friction every day for the life of the building. The hotel will still operate, but it will operate with more steps, more labour, more cross-traffic and more workarounds than necessary.

That is why back-of-house should be treated as part of the operating model, not as leftover space after the public areas have been resolved.

## Start with flows, not rooms

A useful hotel plan begins with movement.

Guests arrive, check in, reach guestrooms, restaurants, meeting rooms and leisure facilities. At the same time, staff arrive, change, clock in, collect equipment, move linen, receive deliveries, remove waste, replenish outlets, service rooms and respond to maintenance calls.

These two systems occupy the same building but should not constantly collide.

The question is not whether every staff route can be invisible. That is rarely realistic. The question is whether routine operational movements can happen efficiently without relying on guest corridors or creating avoidable cross-traffic.

Before refining finishes, trace the main service journeys on the plan:

- loading dock to receiving and stores;
- stores to kitchen and outlets;
- kitchen to restaurant, ballroom and room service dispatch;
- linen and housekeeping stores to guest floors;
- waste from operating areas back to collection;
- engineering workshop to plant rooms and guestrooms;
- staff entrance to lockers, dining and work areas.

If those routes are long, interrupted or dependent on a single bottleneck, the building is already telling you something about its future operating cost.

## Every unnecessary metre becomes labour

Design teams often look at net-to-gross area efficiency. Operators also need to look at travel distance.

A housekeeper walking repeatedly to a distant linen store, a banquet team pushing equipment through a long route or engineering staff crossing several floors for basic tools all create small increments of unproductive time.

Individually, none looks serious. Multiplied across shifts, departments and years, they become part of the labour model.

This is why adjacencies matter.

Housekeeping support should be located around how rooms are actually serviced. Kitchens need practical relationships with receiving, storage, waste and the outlets they serve. Banquet storage should be where banquet equipment is used, not wherever space remained available late in design. Engineering needs access to the systems it maintains.

Good back-of-house design reduces the number of times staff have to compensate for the building.

## Loading and receiving deserve more attention than they get

The loading area is one of the most operationally important parts of a full-service hotel.

Food, beverages, linen, chemicals, engineering parts, furniture, event equipment and general supplies all enter through a relatively small part of the building. Waste leaves through the same service ecosystem.

If vehicle access is awkward, inspection space is limited or receiving is disconnected from storage, the problem does not remain at the loading dock. It moves into corridors, kitchens, stores and delivery schedules.

The design review should consider vehicle turning, security, delivery timing, receiving checks, segregation of food and non-food items, cold-chain requirements where relevant, storage capacity and the route from receipt to final point of use.

A beautiful hotel with a dysfunctional loading sequence will spend years improvising around it.

## Service lifts are capacity decisions

A service lift is not simply a vertical box on a drawing.

Its number, size, speed, location and the departments competing for it matter.

Housekeeping may need to move linen at the same time banqueting is transporting equipment and room service is serving breakfast. Engineering may need to move tools or replacement items during peak periods. Waste movements also need a controlled route.

If the vertical transport strategy is too tight, departments start scheduling around the lift instead of around the guest.

That should be identified during design, not after opening.

## Storage is operational capacity

Storage is often reduced because it does not directly generate revenue.

The logic is understandable. Every square metre has a cost, and owners want as much productive space as possible.

But undersized storage does not eliminate the need to store things. It simply pushes the requirement elsewhere.

Corridors become temporary holding areas. Meeting rooms hold spare furniture. Offices become stock rooms. Housekeeping items appear in service lobbies. Teams order smaller quantities more often because there is nowhere to hold inventory.

The correct level depends on the hotel concept, procurement model, delivery frequency and local supply environment. The important point is to size storage from an operating assumption, not from a generic percentage.

## Design the messy moments

Hotels are easiest to draw when everything is normal.

Operations are defined by the moments when they are not.

A large conference checks in while the ballroom is being reset. Rain drives every arriving guest under the same covered entrance. A guestroom floor has a maintenance incident. A wedding requires unusual staging. A supplier arrives late. Waste collection overlaps with a delivery.

These moments expose whether the service plan has resilience.

During design review, ask teams to run realistic peak scenarios through the drawings. Where do people queue? Where does equipment wait? Which routes conflict? What happens when one lift is unavailable? Can the kitchen support simultaneous outlet and event demand? Can housekeeping stage work without obstructing circulation?

A plan that survives the difficult hour is more valuable than one that only works on a quiet diagram.

## Value engineering needs an operator at the table

Back-of-house space is vulnerable during value engineering because the guest does not directly see the reduction.

A store is made smaller. A corridor narrows. A service door moves. A lift is removed. A staff facility is relocated. Each change may look modest in the capital budget.

The operating effect may be permanent.

That does not mean every original specification should be protected. Hotels have to meet budgets. It means the saving should be assessed against the operating consequence.

The right question is not, “Can we remove this?”

It is, **“If we remove this, how will the hotel operate differently every day?”**

Sometimes the trade-off is sensible. Sometimes a small capital saving creates a recurring labour or service penalty. The owner should know which one it is before approving the change.

## A building should help the team deliver the brand promise

Guests do not compliment a hotel because the service corridor is well planned. They notice the result: rooms ready on time, breakfast replenished smoothly, events reset quickly, luggage moving without confusion and staff appearing where they are needed.

Back-of-house design is therefore not separate from guest experience. It is the infrastructure behind it.

The strongest hotel plans make service feel effortless because the building is doing part of the work.

That is why, when reviewing a hotel design, we would start behind the scenes. If the operation can move efficiently, the guest-facing spaces have a much better chance of performing as intended.
