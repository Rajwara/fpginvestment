Choosing a hotel brand is often discussed as a question of prestige.

Owners should treat it as a question of economics.

A recognised flag can provide distribution, loyalty demand, sales infrastructure, operating standards, technology, training and confidence for lenders and partners. Those benefits can be significant, particularly when an owner is entering a market or segment without an established operating platform.

But they are not free.

Brand relationships may include initial fees, royalties, marketing contributions, reservation charges, loyalty costs, system fees, management fees and capital requirements. They may also constrain how the property is designed, operated, repositioned or sold.

The decision is therefore not, “Is this a good brand?”

It is, **“Is this the right economic and operational partner for this particular asset?”**

## Start with the demand the brand can actually influence

Brand value is market-specific.

A global reservation system can be powerful, but the owner needs to understand how much relevant business the brand is likely to contribute to the hotel’s location and segment.

Look at the sources of demand in the market. Is business primarily international corporate travel, domestic leisure, groups, government, weddings, long stay, airline crew or local events? Does the brand have a strong customer base in those segments? Does its loyalty programme matter to the guests this hotel is trying to attract?

A flag with excellent global recognition may have limited incremental value if the market is overwhelmingly local and relationship-driven. Conversely, an international brand can materially improve access to travellers, corporate accounts or distribution channels that would be expensive for an independent hotel to build alone.

The analysis should be about **incremental demand**, not simply total brand bookings.

## Put every fee into one owner model

Brand proposals can be difficult to compare because fees use different bases.

One may charge a percentage of rooms revenue, another of total revenue. Marketing and reservation charges may be separate or bundled. Loyalty costs can depend on the booking source or programme activity. A management agreement may include a base fee plus an incentive fee. There may also be centralised-service or technology charges.

The owner should convert all of this into a single multi-year model.

Ask:

- What is paid at signing and pre-opening?
- What is paid every month once the hotel is operating?
- Which fees rise with rooms revenue, total revenue or gross operating profit?
- Which costs are fixed regardless of performance?
- What additional systems, staff or compliance costs will the brand require?
- What capital expenditure is required initially and during the contract term?

Only then can the owner compare the cost of affiliation with the revenue and value it may create.

A lower headline royalty does not necessarily mean a lower total cost.

## Distribution has a cost with or without a brand

Independent hotels are not free of distribution expense.

They still need a booking engine, website, channel manager, sales team, digital marketing, public relations, GDS access where relevant, OTA relationships and technology support. They may also need to spend more to create awareness because they do not begin with an established flag.

The useful comparison is therefore not “brand fees versus zero”.

It is **brand system cost versus the cost and effectiveness of building an alternative commercial system**.

This is where some independent hotels are stronger than expected. A property with a distinctive product, strong local reputation, repeat clientele and capable commercial team may be able to generate demand without paying for a global system it only partly uses.

Other assets may struggle to achieve sufficient reach or trust independently.

## Standards can protect value, but they can also require capital

Brand standards are part of what an owner is buying.

They create consistency in design, fire and life safety, technology, service delivery, food and beverage, rooms and operating procedures. They can make a hotel easier to position and can support guest confidence.

But standards also have a physical cost.

During development, a brand may require specifications that affect room size, bathroom design, public areas, back-of-house facilities, building systems and FF&E. For an existing hotel, conversion may require a substantial property-improvement plan.

The question is not whether the standard is good or bad. The question is whether the additional capital is justified by the revenue, operating support and long-term asset value associated with the affiliation.

Owners should identify brand-driven capex early, before they become emotionally committed to the flag.

## Contract terms matter as much as the name above the door

Hotel agreements can run for many years. That makes flexibility valuable.

Owners should understand term, renewal rights, termination provisions, performance tests, territorial restrictions, transfer conditions, approval rights, budget control and obligations at exit.

Two deals with the same brand can create different owner outcomes depending on how those terms are negotiated.

This is also where legal, commercial and operating advice need to meet. A clause can look reasonable legally while creating a difficult operational constraint, or appear acceptable operationally while materially affecting future sale flexibility.

Brand selection should therefore be negotiated alongside the business plan, not after it.

## When a brand tends to create more value

A brand case is usually stronger when several conditions are present:

- the market relies materially on demand sources the brand can reach;
- loyalty and corporate distribution are important to the target guest;
- the owner benefits from established operating systems and standards;
- the property needs credibility with lenders, investors or international partners;
- the brand can support rate positioning that the independent hotel would struggle to achieve;
- the total fee and capex burden still leaves an attractive owner return.

No single point is decisive. The combination matters.

## When independence deserves a serious look

An independent strategy may be stronger when the hotel has a genuinely distinctive product, strong destination demand, a capable operator and a clear route to market without relying heavily on a global brand system.

It can also make sense where brand fees and standards consume more value than the affiliation is likely to create, or where the owner needs greater flexibility to adapt the concept over time.

Independence, however, is not the absence of a brand. It means the owner has to build one.

That requires discipline in positioning, service standards, distribution, reputation management, technology and commercial execution. Saving franchise fees while underinvesting in those capabilities is not a strategy.

## The decision should survive a downside case

Every brand model looks better if optimistic revenue assumptions are used.

Run the downside case.

What happens if occupancy builds more slowly? If ADR is lower? If a major demand segment weakens? If required capex rises? Which fees remain payable? How quickly can the business adapt?

The strongest brand decision is one that still makes sense when the first few years are harder than planned.

Ultimately, the flag should be treated like any other investment in the hotel: it needs to earn its place.

A good brand can create value well beyond its fee. The wrong one can become an expensive long-term commitment.

The owner’s job is to know the difference before signing.
