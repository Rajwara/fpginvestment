A hotel P&L can tell you that profit missed budget.

It cannot, by itself, tell you what to do on Monday morning.

That is the difference between reading a financial statement as a report and reading it as an operator.

An operator is looking for cause and effect. Which revenue moved? What kind of business created it? What cost should have moved with it? Which expense is genuinely structural, and which one reflects a controllable operating decision? Is a poor percentage being caused by overspending or simply by weak revenue?

Gross operating profit matters, but the useful work begins one level below it.

## Start with revenue quality, not total revenue

Two months can produce the same rooms revenue through very different operating outcomes.

One month may have stronger occupancy at a lower average rate. Another may have fewer occupied rooms at a higher rate. The revenue line is similar, but housekeeping workload, laundry, utilities, breakfast covers, commissions and guest-acquisition cost may be different.

That is why rooms revenue should be read with occupancy, ADR, RevPAR, segment mix and channel cost.

Ask:

- Which segments grew or declined?
- Did higher occupancy come from profitable business?
- Was rate protected on high-demand dates?
- How much revenue was generated through higher-cost channels?
- Did complimentary rooms, packages or inclusions distort the apparent performance?
- Were all available rooms actually sellable?

The P&L becomes more useful when the commercial story is attached to it.

## Read departmental profit before GOP

A hotel is several operating businesses sharing one building.

Rooms, food and beverage, spa, parking and other operated departments have different cost structures. Looking only at total hotel profit can hide a weak department behind a strong one.

For rooms, examine labour, guest supplies, laundry, commissions and reservation-related costs against occupancy and revenue. For food and beverage, look at outlet-level revenue where possible, cost of sales, payroll, covers, average check and the economics of banqueting separately from restaurants.

The question is not simply whether an expense is over budget.

It is whether the department converted its revenue into profit at the level the operating model should reasonably produce.

## Labour needs an operating denominator

Payroll is often one of the largest controllable cost categories in a hotel, but payroll percentage alone can mislead.

If revenue falls sharply, payroll as a percentage of revenue can deteriorate even when headcount has not changed. That does not mean management should automatically cut people. It means the owner needs to understand productivity and scheduling.

Useful measures include labour cost per occupied room, hours per occupied room, staffing by cover or event, overtime, agency usage and full-time-equivalent trends.

Then look at the roster.

Did staffing flex with occupancy? Were departments holding fixed schedules during weak periods? Is overtime caused by vacancies, poor planning or unusually strong demand? Is a department understaffed in a way that is generating guest complaints and future revenue risk?

A labour variance is an operational conversation, not just a finance comment.

## Flow-through shows what happened to incremental revenue

One of the most useful questions in a strong revenue month is: **How much of the additional revenue reached profit?**

That is the logic behind flow-through.

If revenue is materially ahead of budget but profit barely improves, costs have absorbed the gain. Sometimes that is expected because the revenue came from a labour-intensive department or required high acquisition cost. Sometimes it reveals poor control.

The reverse is also useful. When revenue falls, flex analysis helps show how much cost the hotel was able to remove or avoid.

These measures force management to connect top-line performance with the cost of producing it.

Revenue growth is valuable. Profitable revenue growth is the real objective.

## Out-of-order rooms belong in the financial conversation

A room that cannot be sold is a physical defect with a commercial consequence.

If a hotel repeatedly carries rooms out of order, the owner should see more than an engineering note. The impact appears through lost available inventory, potential lost revenue, guest moves, upgrade pressure and inefficient room assignment.

Track the number of rooms, the reason, the age of each issue and the revenue periods affected.

This is a good example of why financial review should not stay inside the finance department. A maintenance problem can become a revenue-management problem, a housekeeping problem and a guest-experience problem at the same time.

## Undistributed costs need owners, too

Administrative and general, sales and marketing, property operations and maintenance, utilities, and information and telecommunications costs sit outside the directly operated departments, but they are not beyond operational control.

Look for trends, not only monthly variances.

Utilities may reflect tariff changes, weather, occupancy or plant efficiency. Sales and marketing may be ahead of budget because of a planned campaign, but the owner should still know what the spend is expected to produce. Repairs may be low because preventive maintenance is excellent, or because necessary work is being deferred.

A favourable variance is not always good news.

That is why explanations matter.

## Compare against three things, not one

Budget is only one reference point.

A useful monthly review compares actual performance with:

1. **Budget** – what management planned to achieve.
2. **Prior period / prior year** – what changed over time.
3. **Relevant operational or market benchmark** – whether performance is strong relative to the environment.

Budget can be wrong. Last year can be abnormal. A market benchmark can also require interpretation because no two hotels have identical facilities or positioning.

Used together, however, they help the owner avoid drawing conclusions from a single comparison.

## Ask for a bridge, not an excuse

When GOP is behind budget, management should be able to bridge the variance in plain language.

For example:

- rooms revenue was down because corporate demand softened;
- rate held, but occupancy declined;
- banquet revenue partly offset the gap;
- payroll did not flex quickly enough in two departments;
- utilities were above plan because of tariff movement and higher cooling load;
- three rooms remained out of order longer than expected;
- corrective actions are already assigned.

That is much more useful than a narrative saying the market was difficult.

A good owner report turns the P&L into decisions: what happened, why, what is controllable, who owns the response and when the effect should appear.

## Five questions an owner can ask every month

1. Which revenue movement had the biggest impact on profit, and what caused it?
2. Which department converted revenue into profit better or worse than expected?
3. Did labour flex with the actual level and mix of business?
4. How much incremental revenue flowed through to GOP, or how much cost flexed when revenue fell?
5. Which operational issue in this month’s numbers could become a bigger problem next quarter if we leave it alone?

The P&L is not the end of the operating conversation. It is where the most useful questions begin.

Read properly, it tells the owner where to look. The operator’s job is to connect those numbers back to rooms, guests, people, systems and decisions, then change the ones that can still be changed.
