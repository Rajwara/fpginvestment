When a hotel underperforms, renovation is an attractive answer.

It is visible. It feels decisive. It gives the owner and team a project to rally around.

It can also be the wrong first move.

An asset may have tired rooms, but it may also be pricing badly, carrying the wrong business mix, losing sellable inventory to maintenance issues, overstaffing quiet periods, underinvesting in sales or paying too much for distribution. A refurbishment will not correct those problems by itself.

The first ninety days of a turnaround should therefore be about diagnosis, control and sequencing.

The aim is not to fix everything immediately. It is to understand what is actually driving performance, stop avoidable leakage and build enough operational stability to make larger decisions with evidence rather than frustration.

## Days 1–15: establish the truth

The first job is to create a reliable baseline.

Management reports often contain plenty of data but not always a clear explanation of why the hotel is underperforming. Start by reconciling the commercial, operational and financial story.

Look at:

- occupancy, ADR and RevPAR by segment and channel;
- room-type performance and upgrade behaviour;
- pace, pickup and booking-window trends;
- cancellation and no-show patterns;
- payroll by department and by occupied room;
- departmental margins;
- commissions and distribution cost;
- out-of-order and out-of-service rooms;
- guest complaints and recurring service failures;
- maintenance backlog;
- major contracts and purchasing terms;
- sales-account production;
- cash controls, receivables and purchasing discipline.

Then walk the hotel.

A spreadsheet may show 180 keys. The operation may only have 169 reliably sellable tonight. The P&L may show a high payroll percentage, but the real issue may be weak revenue rather than an excessive headcount. Food cost may look poor because of purchasing, wastage, pricing, portion control or incorrect revenue capture.

The first two weeks are about separating symptoms from causes.

## Days 15–30: stop the leaks

Once the baseline is credible, act on issues that can improve performance without waiting for a major capital programme.

Revenue management is usually high on that list.

Check whether pricing changes with demand, whether discounts are controlled, whether lower-rated business is displacing better demand on compression dates and whether channel strategy reflects the true cost of acquisition. Review room-type premiums and restrictions. Identify corporate accounts that produce volume but little contribution after discounts and inclusions.

At the same time, recover sellable inventory.

An out-of-order room is not only an engineering issue. It is a revenue asset that has temporarily disappeared. Some rooms will require capital work, but others may be recoverable quickly with disciplined maintenance, parts procurement or clearer accountability.

The same principle applies to costs. Review overtime, agency labour, purchasing leakage, unused subscriptions, service contracts and approval limits. The goal is not an indiscriminate cost-cut. Cutting service in a weak hotel can accelerate decline. The goal is to remove cost that does not support the guest or the revenue plan.

## Days 30–60: reset commercial discipline

By the second month, the hotel should have a clearer view of where demand comes from and where it is missing.

This is the point to rebuild the commercial rhythm.

A useful weekly meeting does not simply report occupancy. It looks forward.

What has changed in the next 30, 60 and 90 days? Which need periods are still exposed? What business is on the books by segment? Which accounts have stopped producing? What events are entering the market? Where is the hotel priced against its competitive set? Which channels are contributing profitable demand rather than just volume?

Sales activity also needs evidence. Account lists should show production, potential and next action. Group enquiries should be evaluated on total value and displacement, not just room nights. Marketing spend should connect to a defined audience and booking path.

Turnaround work often improves when commercial and operational teams stop treating revenue as somebody else’s department.

## Days 45–75: rebuild operating standards

Commercial improvement will not hold if the guest experience is inconsistent.

By now the team should know the most common service failures and where they originate. Focus first on recurring processes rather than isolated mistakes.

If rooms are not ready on time, examine staffing, room assignment, linen flow, maintenance release and communication between front office and housekeeping. If breakfast complaints repeat, look at forecasting, prep levels, buffet replenishment, seating and peak staffing. If maintenance requests remain open, examine how work orders are logged, prioritised, completed and closed.

Standard operating procedures matter, but only when they describe how the hotel really works. A turnaround is a good time to simplify them and reconnect department heads to daily controls.

Training should follow the same logic. Do not begin with broad classroom programmes. Begin with the failure points that are costing revenue, guest confidence or staff time now.

## Days 60–90: decide what deserves capital

After two months of disciplined operation, the renovation conversation becomes more useful.

Some physical issues will clearly require investment. Rooms may need refurbishment. Public spaces may be dated. Mechanical systems may be unreliable. Brand standards may require a property-improvement plan.

But the owner can now rank capital needs against operating evidence.

Which defects are taking rooms out of inventory? Which issues are repeatedly mentioned by guests? Which spaces limit revenue? Which equipment is increasing labour or utility cost? Which improvements are cosmetic, and which change the asset’s competitive position?

This is a much stronger basis for capital planning than “the hotel feels tired”.

Capital should support a defined operating and positioning strategy. Otherwise the owner risks spending money to produce a better-looking version of the same weak business model.

## The management team is part of the diagnostic

A turnaround is not only a review of numbers and assets. It is also a review of decision-making.

Some underperforming hotels have capable teams working inside unclear structures. Others have leadership gaps that no process improvement can fully compensate for.

The first ninety days should establish whether department heads understand their numbers, whether meetings lead to decisions, whether actions have owners and deadlines, and whether performance is challenged consistently.

Changing people too quickly can destroy useful knowledge. Avoiding necessary leadership changes can stall the turnaround. The evidence should guide the decision.

## What owners should expect by day ninety

Not a finished turnaround.

A hotel that has struggled for years will not be transformed in three months. What should exist is control.

The owner should have:

- a credible operating baseline;
- a short list of the largest revenue and cost opportunities;
- a functioning revenue-management rhythm;
- a plan to return viable rooms and facilities to service;
- clearer departmental accountability;
- defined service priorities;
- a ranked capital plan tied to operational need;
- a 12-month forecast built on revised assumptions.

The most important output is clarity about sequence.

Fix what can be fixed through management first. Invest where capital genuinely changes performance. Measure whether the interventions are working. Then keep adjusting.

A hotel turnaround is rarely one dramatic decision. It is a series of disciplined decisions made in the right order.
